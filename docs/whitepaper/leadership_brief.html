Leadership Brief · v1.1

PE Delivery Analytics: what it tells us, how far to trust it, and how it stays safe

By Jorge E. Rodriguez, M.Sc. · Platform Engineering · October 2026 (updated Oct 21; first edition Oct 8) · Companion to the technical white paper

Bottom line
  • One dashboard, built only on our Jira data, answers five leadership questions: are we meeting our SLAs, when will work finish, how much can we take on, who should do what, and how is the work connected.
  • Forecasts were tested against what actually happened: the dates we tell people to commit to are met about as often as promised (93% vs a target of 85%), and the 4-week delivery forecast landed inside its range 80% of the time.
  • New: a caution on SLA compliance. For 35% of recently completed tickets, the SLA start date was entered only after the work was finished, and every one of them counts as "met". Compliance looks better than it is (page 2).
  • New: relationship graphs show hidden dependencies on single people and stalled work we can unblock today (page 3).
  • The dashboard only recommends. It changes Jira only when an engineer turns writing on, on their own computer, reviews and confirms. Every change is logged, commented in Jira and can be undone.

The questions it answers

1. Are we meeting our commitments? SLA compliance and two breach rates against our goal of under 10% breached, the trend, and the tickets that are late or about to be.SLA · Executive Summary
2. When will work finish, and start? A likely date and a safe date to commit to for every ticket in progress or in the backlog. In Progress · Backlog
3. How much can we take on? Tickets the team will likely deliver in 4, 8 and 12 weeks with an honest range, and a "can we commit to this project?" calculator.Delivery Forecast · Capacity
4. Who should do what, and what slows us down? A suggested owner for each new ticket with backup and reason; the friction in comments that costs the most time. Suggested Assignments · Teams Conversations
5. How is the work connected? (new) Who asks whom, who talks to whom, who holds which skills, and which tickets wait on others, including across teams. Knowledge Graph · plus a new Welcome page that guides first-time users to the right page

How it works, in plain terms

  • One source, one set of definitions. Every page counts tickets, completions and SLA dates the same way.
  • Business days, like our SLAs. Weekends and company holidays are skipped everywhere.
  • Learns from our own history. Forecasts come from how similar tickets actually went for this team.
  • Ranges, not single dates. "Likely" is a coin flip; "safe" is what to commit to.
  • Explainable. Every number traces back to tickets. No AI writes the text; fixed, tested rules do.
  • Checks its own inputs. Jira's change history is used to catch data that would flatter the numbers.

A caution on SLA compliance

The SLA clock starts at a ticket's Target start. Jira's history shows that 102 of the 293 tickets completed in the last 30 days (35%) had their Target start entered on or after the day the work was done, usually at closing. All 102 count as "met", because a clock started at the finish cannot run out. The share has grown from 5–6% last winter to 30–32% since July.

Why it matters: leaving those tickets out raises today's breach rates from 4.2% to 6.4% and from 4.4% to 6.8%, still under the 10% goal. Two weeks ago the same check moved one rate from 7.5% to 12.1%, above the goal.
Fix: require a Target start before a ticket can move to In Progress. The SLA page now flags these tickets and can leave them out.

Safeguards

RiskProtection
Unintended changes to JiraWriting is off by default and only works on the engineer's own computer. Nothing is pre-selected; changes are previewed and need a typed confirmation.
Overwriting a colleague's editEach ticket is re-checked just before the change; if someone changed it meanwhile, it is skipped.
Changes nobody can traceEvery change adds a Jira comment and an audit-log entry.
A batch that turns out wrongOne-step undo, which never overrides someone's later edits.
Flattering the SLAMoved dates are judged on the original start; clocks set after the fact are flagged.
Our own comments skewing metricsComments the dashboard posts are excluded from comment coverage and response times.
Assigning the wrong personAssignment by Jira account, never by name; departed members are never suggested; new members are.
Misusing people views and graphsFor balancing work and cross-training, not rating people; graphs in our documents are anonymised.

Limits to keep in mind

  • It is only as good as Jira. Missing sizes and late-entered start dates distort SLA results (see page 2).
  • Graphs and comment themes show association, not proof. Conversations outside Jira aren't seen.
  • The assignment suggestions are new. Their effect on SLA results still has to be measured.

What we recommend

  1. Require Target start before In Progress, so every SLA clock starts when work is planned.
  2. Commit to requesters using "safe" dates, not likely ones.
  3. Post forecasts weekly (all backlog) to build an unbiased track record.
  4. Cross-train ECS and Looker; name a backup for the 6 single-engineer requesters.
  5. Build direct links between groups to reduce reliance on one connector.
  6. Clear the 8 "can likely move" tickets.
  7. Pilot Suggested Assignments and compare SLA results for followed vs not-followed suggestions.
